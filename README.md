## Building a Professional Brand Without Giving Up Your Privacy

When I created my LinkedIn profile, I found myself thinking about a question I had never really considered before.

How much of my professional life actually needs to be public?

LinkedIn is one of the best platforms ever created for professional networking. It helps people connect with experts, discover new opportunities, learn from each other, and share their work. I genuinely believe it has changed many careers for the better.

At the same time, I also think we have slowly accepted an idea that deserves to be questioned.

Many of us now feel that being professional means making our entire employment history public.

Every employer.

Every date.

Every career move.

Every transition.

But should it?

I don't think there is a single correct answer. Every person should decide for themselves. My point is simply that we should make this decision consciously, rather than because everyone else does.

For me, there is a difference between **building a professional brand** and **publishing a professional archive**.

A professional profile should help people understand what I can do.

How I think.

What I build.

What problems I enjoy solving.

What technologies I work with.

How I continue learning.

Those things say much more about me than a complete timeline of every company I have worked for.

Careers are not linear.

People change industries.

People relocate.

People take breaks.

Sometimes a project fails.

Sometimes a company changes direction.

Sometimes life simply takes us somewhere unexpected.

None of those moments reduce a person's professional value.

That is why I believe our digital identity should focus more on our capabilities than on our timeline.

Projects.

Ideas.

Articles.

Open-source contributions.

Technical discussions.

Continuous learning.

These are the things I personally want my professional profile to highlight.

This is not a criticism of LinkedIn.

Nor do I think employment history is unimportant.

For many people it is relevant, and sharing it is absolutely the right choice.

My point is different.

I simply believe that professionals should have complete freedom to decide how much of their history becomes permanently public.

Professional networking should create opportunities.

It should not create pressure to share more personal information than someone feels comfortable sharing.

As software engineers, we often talk about privacy, security, and user control when we build systems.

Perhaps we should apply the same principles to our own digital identities.

Technology should give people more control over their information, not less.

That is why I prefer to build my professional presence around the things I create, rather than the places I have been.

My work.

My projects.

My ideas.

My curiosity.

Because in the end, those are the things that continue to grow long after a job title has changed.

## My Personal Principles

- Share knowledge.
- Share ideas.
- Share projects.
- Keep learning.
- Respect privacy.
- Let your work speak louder than your timeline.
